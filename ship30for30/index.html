Shipping 30 atomic essays in 30 days

In January 2021, I'm taking part in Dickie Bush's Ship 30 for 30 writing programme, where we write 30 "atomic essays" in 30 days.

The format of the programme involves publishing text as images on Twitter, but for accessibility purposes I'm going to post here as well.

Day 1: It’s the notes you don’t play

Guitarists are notorious for obsessing over their gear. When B.B King found the perfect axe, he was so in love he gave it the affectionate name ‘Lucille’. Eric Clapton, knowing some of his instruments could never be replaced, risked his life to rescue all he could carry from a house fire.

So it’s odd that Jack White centred The White Stripes’ sound around what he calls “a hollow piece of plastic”: a budget guitar from a sixties mail order catalogue. It won’t stay in tune and, as he puts it, “if you want it easy, buy a brand new Les Paul”.

But White’s choice was born of a desire to give himself a creative constraint. He’s explained that making things a little difficult for himself forced him to think in different ways, having to write simpler songs or use alternate tunings.

~~~

When I first considered the idea of publishing “atomic essays” to Twitter as images, it felt like picking up a guitar that wouldn’t stay in tune. Why do that when there are much more sophisticated options? I felt 250 words too trivial to say anything useful. I nerd-worried that they couldn’t be crawled by search engines, and knew lack of hypertext would stop me linking to the Wikipedia pages of big ideas I’m too lazy to explain.

But when writing, sometimes I can be a too-many-notes shredder. So for the next 30 days, I’m giving myself some creative constraints.

Day 2: #blessed

When I took my first mindfulness classes seven years ago, I was a sceptic.

I’m data-minded for a living, so anything that comes even close to New Age has to pass an empirical sniff test.

Jon Kabat-Zinn’s one of the key people responsible for popularising meditation in the West over the past forty years. Hearing him talk on the subject, backed by his PhD in molecular biology from MIT, convinced me it was worth sticking with the eight-week course. I’ve never looked back.

But if mindfulness sounded a little bit out there at first, the phrase ‘gratitude journal’ downright sounded like a Gwyneth Paltrow stationery product.

Having already become an evangelist of something I’d once dismissed as ‘woo’, I wondered if it could happen again. I found a specific bit of research on sensorimotor feedback from handwriting that helped. This can be what makes the difference between simply thinking words and really internalising their meaning. That was enough to get me to try.

Years later, I can’t tell you I sit at an ornate wooden desk every evening and do my best Samuel Pepys impression. And I’m always reluctant to talk about it online as, out of context, genuine gratitude can seem like Instagram showboating. But occasionally, when I’m not feeling my best, I return to listing out in longhand on pen and paper a few things I’m grateful for right then.

It’s become a vital practical tool for shifting my perspective and improving my mood.

Day 3: Wikipedia gave me a maxim to live by

Some people knit or play sudoku while watching TV. I’ve spent my recent evenings keeping an eye on Wikipedia’s changelog while watching YouTube.

The vast majority of English Wikipedia’s 150,000+ daily edits do improve the encyclopaedia. But, examples of the less-than-ideal ones I encountered:

  • Replacing Boris Johnson’s name with a few, ahem, choice words in the ‘Parliament of the United Kingdom’ article.
  • Enthusiastically expanding ‘The Boy in the Striped Pyjamas (film)’ with a fair but unencyclopaedic critique of its treatment of Nazism.
  • Adding unreferenced, surprising, but possibly true news to the biography of actress Tanya Roberts (is she dead or alive?!).
  • And sometimes: off-by-one-digit numerical typos in important stats on scientific and political articles, added by experienced editors, with references to the correct figures.

Of the imperfect edits, some are vandalism, plain and simple. But some damaging ones are well-meaning. Some that add useful facts and encyclopaedic value still need a little help along the way. And mistakes do happen.

Wikipedia’s guidelines for editors have a core principle that one should “assume good faith”. They go on: “Most people try to help the project, not hurt it. If this were untrue, a project like Wikipedia would be doomed from the beginning.”

They explain that unless there’s obvious evidence to the contrary (as with that positively Swiftian jab at the UK’s PM), editors should try to understand each other’s intentions before edit warring.

And isn’t that a good metaphor for life?

Day 4: The Devil's Data Dictionary

With apologies to Ambrose Bierce, satirist and author of 1906 classic The Devil's Dictionary.

~~~

ALGORITHM, n. A set of instructions written by humans, to which many somehow ascribe magical powers. A good scapegoat for the biases of its authors.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE, n. What the CEO calls your pile of nested IF statements in a shell script.

ATTRIBUTE, v. To figure out what happened one click before.

BENCHMARK, n. A quick way to see, without thinking too hard, if you're in trouble or not.

CAUSAL, adj. See CORRELATIVE.

CUSTOMER, n. A unique identifier in a database. Nothing more. Right?

DASHBOARD, n. A type of data visualisation commonly requested when no-one knows what question they should be asking.

EXCEL, n. World's most popular enterprise data management platform.

EXPONENTIALLY, adj. Smart-sounding way to say "lots". Has no specific mathematical meaning.
